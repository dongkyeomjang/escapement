# ① Introduction

Agentic workloads interleave LLM calls with tool execution. A session issues a request, receives a response, runs a tool, and comes back. The serving cost of such a workload is not set by the requests alone: it is set by **when the sessions come back**, because that arrival process decides how many of them are resident at once, and the accelerator charges for residency. <!-- CLAIMS 1.1, 1.5, 1.8 -->

This paper decomposes that return-arrival process on real hardware — an RBLN CA25 NPU running `vllm 0.22.0` with `vllm-rbln 0.11.1` and artifacts built by `optimum-rbln 0.11.1` — and finds three mechanisms that turn it into device time. None of the three responds to a property of an individual request; all three respond to a *collective* property of arrivals. <!-- CLAIMS 1.1, 1.5, 1.8 -->

The substrate is one instance of a broader class rather than a curiosity. Accelerators that fix tensor shapes when the model is built, and therefore enumerate a **discrete set** of them, include AWS Inferentia [@neuronbucketing] — where input shape is fixed at compile time and bucketing is the documented remedy for variable sizes — and Intel Gaudi [@gaudishapes], whose graph compiler prefers static shapes and recompiles when they change. The same discreteness appears inside GPU serving stacks as the enumerated CUDA-graph capture sizes, and Section ⑦ shows from the serving stack's own source that the alignment law studied here does **not** vanish there. <!-- CLAIMS 4.1, 4.2 --> What follows is measured on one member of that class; which statements are about the class and which about the member is marked throughout.

First, the accelerator does not decode an arbitrary batch width: it rounds the running set up to one of a **discrete set of compiled widths**. Whether the offered concurrency lands on a rung or between two rungs determines not only the magnitude but also the **sign** of the effect that a tool gap has [[fig:fig2]]. <!-- CLAIMS 1.5 --> We establish this causally rather than by correlation, with a recompile that changes only the grid and holds workload, seed, model and slot count fixed. <!-- CLAIMS 1.6 -->

Second, completed prefixes live in a pool of **sequence-granular slots**, and when the pool is full the oldest allocation is evicted whole. Survival is therefore a cliff rather than a slope, and its threshold counts *requests*, not tokens. <!-- CLAIMS 1.1 --> On this instance a completed prefix remains cached through six background requests and is evicted at the seventh [[fig:fig1]], because the layer-2 pool holds eight outer slots of 8,192 tokens each under FIFO; the threshold is `8 − 1 − 1`, and it reproduced 12/12 deterministically. <!-- CLAIMS 1.2 -->

Third, prefill runs **exclusively**: while one session recomputes a prefix, every other session's decode is stalled for the full duration [[fig:fig3]]. The cost of a cache miss is therefore not that session's latency but recomputation time multiplied by the number of concurrent decoders. <!-- CLAIMS 1.8 --> This is where the first two mechanisms multiply: the grid decides how wide the batch is, the slot pool decides who has to recompute, and exclusive prefill charges the recomputation to everyone.

## Position with respect to prior work

Two of the ideas this paper stands on are already in the literature. **LENS** [@lens2026] makes explicit that an NPU's discrete bucket grid renders latency non-linear, and makes that non-linearity predictable; **AgentServeSim** [@agentservesim2026] builds a simulator for multi-turn agent serving — KV residency across tool gaps included — precisely because the policy space is too large to measure. Neither treating the grid as a first-class object nor simulating agent serving originates here. The two stop on different axes, however. LENS sees the grid but sees *one request*, so the cross-session dependency — where one session's KV-slot occupancy determines another session's cache survival — is undefined in its model. AgentServeSim sees multi-session dynamics but targets a **dynamic GPU runtime** — continuous batching, tunable reclaim, a memory hierarchy — and so does not ask what remains when reclaim is a sequence-granular FIFO frozen at compile time. **This paper sits at the intersection**: an NPU's compile-static grid meeting a multi-session arrival process. That intersection was empty, and it is where four fifths of the device time we recover comes from. The chain runs from `batch_size` to KV slot count to cache survival to prefill recomputation, and it appears neither in a single-request model nor in one that presumes reclaim policy can be changed. <!-- CLAIMS 3.6, 3.8 --> Our simulator's contribution is likewise not that a simulator exists, but that it predicts the device time of a configuration **that has not yet been compiled, with zero fitted parameters, from predictions committed before measurement** — which is the only thing that licenses taking device measurement out of the selection loop. <!-- CLAIMS 1.15, 3.2, 3.14 -->

## What this paper reports

On top of the three mechanisms we build a step-level model with **no fitted parameters**, and put its predictions through preregistered gates: it reproduces eighty previously measured combinations, then predicts out-of-sample cells committed before measurement [[fig:fig4]], then predicts cells in which a control intervention was applied, then survives a change of the gap distribution itself. <!-- CLAIMS 1.14, 1.15, 1.16, 1.17 -->

With that model we obtain a **negative result**, and it is the reason the paper's positive result takes the shape it does. Rescheduling return times leaves real offline headroom, but a median 60 % of that headroom is *coordination* [[fig:fig5]]: give every session omniscient knowledge and let each decide independently, and that share disappears. **No per-session runtime policy can reach it, in principle.** <!-- CLAIMS 2.5, 2.6 --> Nor is the remainder reachable in practice: the value of return-time information is workload-specific and vanishes under measured tool latencies <!-- CLAIMS 2.7 -->, and a published duration estimator, borrowed verbatim, misses the accuracy threshold by a margin that four additional orders of magnitude in sample size do not close [[fig:fig6]]. <!-- CLAIMS 2.8, 2.9 -->

That leaves one reachable lever: a **compile-time configuration**, which is coordination decided once, in advance, for everybody. <!-- CLAIMS 2.6, 3.1 --> We select one from workload *distribution* statistics and the uncalibrated model alone, with no device measurement in the selection loop, apply it with an eight-minute recompile, and confirm the recovery on two independent channels registered before measurement [[fig:fig8]]. <!-- CLAIMS 3.2, 3.3, 3.4 -->

Finally we ask where the lever stops. Pushing `batch_size` from 8 to 32 shows the gain **saturating at 16** and going flat [[fig:fig9]], for a reason the data makes visible: the survival rate has reached 100 %, so a larger pool has no cache left to save, and the widest compiled rung is never selected at this concurrency, so its higher step cost is never charged either. <!-- CLAIMS 3.10, 3.11, 3.12 --> The KV ceiling on this device lies near `B = 46`, three times beyond where the gain ends — **the optimal configuration is set by the workload distribution, not by what the hardware can hold.** <!-- CLAIMS 3.13 -->

## Contributions

1. A device-level decomposition of the return-arrival process into three mechanisms, with the first established **causally by a recompile intervention**. <!-- CLAIMS 1.5, 1.6 -->
2. A step-level substrate model with **zero fitted parameters** that passes preregistered out-of-sample, intervention and workload-transfer gates. <!-- CLAIMS 1.14–1.17 -->
3. An information decomposition showing that most of the available headroom is **coordination**, which per-session runtime policy cannot reach even with omniscient knowledge. <!-- CLAIMS 2.5, 2.6 -->
4. A compile-time configuration selected from distribution statistics alone and **confirmed on device** against preregistered predictions, together with the saturation curve that bounds it. <!-- CLAIMS 3.4, 3.5, 3.11 -->
